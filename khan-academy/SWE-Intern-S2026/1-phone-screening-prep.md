**Problem-solving under pressure or uncertainty.** 

*How you break down ambiguous problems?*

When I come across ambiguous problems, I first do as much research into them as I can. I like to break a problem into subproblems and plan it out, and so knowing as much as I can from that would be ideal. Once I am hopefully more knowledgeable on the problem at hand, I would ask for clarification from my superiors or others possibly working on the same problem to gain more perspective on areas of uncertainty, clearing things up as much as I can. At the point where I have the most context and knowledge of what I am solving, I would then break it down into actionable steps, solving smaller subproblems, which would then trickle back up to coming together and solving the entire issue. Under pressure, I think this is important to track your progress along the way and make it easier, not just for you to work on, but also for possible collaboration. It allows for the passing of tasks to others in a clear outline, reducing any further ambiguity from occurring.



*Describe a time when you disagreed with a teammate. How did you resolve it?*

During my previous internship, I had a disagreement with a teammate on the services we were going to use to host the main application for the company. I leaned more towards using AWS, for the full customization and control it had, as well as the large scale support it would provide for the estimated scale the CEO and CTO had envisioned. However, he had concerns about possible costs from hosting on AWS compared to something like Render or Heroku. The budget constraints were incredibly vague, as our superiors had no idea what ranges they were willing to spend per month on all hosting services for the application, server, and database. It was then up to the two of us to decide and implement that. To get through the conflict and end up on the same page again, I chose to have a sit-down with him, and list out all the pros and cons of each, as well as prioritizations of certain factors. A big part of my hesitance to use Render and Heroku were the startup times. Compared to always-on hosting like AWS EC2 or ECS, there would be a significant cold start time, something the CTO wanted to minimize. However, those options would have costed more than necessary for the beginning, since the company had not yet garnered enough users, which was his worry. After going through the different alternatives, the two of us ended up reaching an agreement on the use of AWS Lambda for the server-side, directly tying in with AWS CloudFront and S3 for the static page distribution. This way, we would still minimize start up times, since Lambda’s cold start times were much faster than its counterparts. However, we would still minimize costs during no-usage downtimes, which would have reduced a lot of unnecessary uptime for its few users. The launch went smoothly, and it reinforced for me that collaboration means aligning technical decisions with shared priorities, not just technical preferences.


*How do you handle feedback?*

I enjoy getting feedback, especially constructive criticism. I think it is really important to   have another perspective on your work to gain a larger exposure to what you are doing and the effect it has, as opposed to the effect you think it has. In my opinion, learning from constructive criticism, especially that of more senior co-workers, is the best way to keep improving in any given area. I have a specific process in which I handle feedback: Firstly, I receive and try to understand the feedback that is being given, like what do they fully mean when they say that. I then try to go back into code I have written, or past interactions, and point out where I made some errors or inefficient decisions. If I get any blocks in figuring them out, I then consult with others, ideally those that gave me feedback. From that, I draft out how to improve on those areas, bringing them up and discussing them. Once it has been possibly revised and ready to implement, I then change wherever necessary, and use what I have learned and apply that in the future. I use this pipeline because it allows me to try and figure out where I went wrong, while not taking too much time to do so. I believe actually doing is far better for retention than just being told, and so fixing the mistakes not only allows for ownership and accountability for grey areas, but helps me focus in on the errors and try to make sure I learn from the past. By discussing the proposed changes and such with others, I can align what I believe is the right correction with the expectations and standards of the team as well, ensuring that my code quality and communication are improved and won’t receive this feedback anymore. In my eyes, the process is an iterative way to improve oneself, basically aiming to reduce mistakes/errors one after another carefully and intentionally. 


*Have you ever had to explain a technical concept to someone less technical?*

Yes, during a meeting with the CTO and CEO of ForOurLastNames, I explained the architecture of the application, how it matched with the hosting selections we chose, and explained the overall pipeline to them as well. The application itself is a single-origin static page application, and so I simplified that as a whiteboard and person. The whiteboard was effectively the static page application, using one board (the root index.html file) to display the contents of each static page based on what was happening. The contents were being rendered on the same whiteboard, just as the pages were being rendered on the same index.html routing file. Additionally, I chose to have the whiteboard-writer setup to show the single origin aspect. The erasing and writing of contents onto the whiteboard was done by the person, and the thinking was also done by the person. I thought this made it easy to visualize how intertwined the process was, being sent through the same person and having two different routes: one to write to the whiteboard and another to think. I then tied this directly to AWS CloudFront. Each request went through CloudFront, either going to writing on the whiteboard, or displaying pages through S3, or to the person to think, effectively representing the lambda function. I was able to deliver a clean visual and understanding of the structure of our hosted application, syncing the CTO and CEO with the visualization the team had. Ultimately, it taught me the importance of simplification and the power of analogies to convey concepts and teach them, something that I believe would be applicable to Khan Academy.

**Problem-Solving and Learning**

*Tell me about a challenging bug you encountered. How did you debug it?*

One challenging bug I encountered was the persistence of sessions and managing sessions for XSRF attack protection during the creation of my project, StockSense. I was trying to maintain session to validate with the backend for the XSRF tokens, however, I kept getting mismatching sessions in the backend and browser. I spent nearly 2 weeks trying to debug it, going back and forth, trying various different methods to align the sessions. Ultimately, I discovered that the issue was with new sessions being created and stored for each group of Chrome tabs that was opening. From there, I implemented HTTPS session management through Redis, having it sync the sessions and store them for me. From that, I was able to properly validate each request for XSRF protection. IN the process, I learned to be open to external tools. Had I explored more besides trying to fix it manually myself, I might have been able to implement the Redis session management sooner, and saved myself more time to focus on more testing or creating new features.
